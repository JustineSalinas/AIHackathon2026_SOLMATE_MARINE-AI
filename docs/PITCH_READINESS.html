03What it solves

A bolt-on kit for the traditional diesel passenger boats already working in the Philippines. No new boat, no new engine — sensors and a screen added to a boat that is already in service. Three sets of sensors feed three separate AI systems, and all three answers appear on one screen at the helm.

Boats we could reach
~5,000+
wooden and fiberglass diesel passenger boats running between PH islands
How it installs
Bolt-on
fits boats already in the water — no new hulls needed
Who decides
The captain

Who it serves
3 groups
captain (screen) · owner (savings report) · government & lenders (exported evidence)

Problem 1 — Fuel is the biggest cost a boat owner can control, and captains guess the throttle Working

What we do: work out the cheapest engine speed that still gets you there on time. The system holds the promised arrival time fixed and tries every throttle setting to find the one that burns least fuel while still arriving on schedule. It never simply tells you to go slower.

The product is the saving, not the number. Not "run at 1650 RPM" but "1650 RPM saves you 2.1 litres per hour."

RPM = engine revolutions per minute, the number on the boat's rev counter. It's the unit a captain actually controls with the throttle lever.

Problem 1b — The same fuel question, but about which path to take Working

What we do: draw several possible paths between the two ports — the straight one, plus versions that swing wider to each side. Each path is chopped into short segments, the expected weather is looked up for each segment, and the fuel cost of every segment is worked out using the same calculation as the throttle advice above.

Two things can reject a path outright rather than just making it expensive: water that is too shallow (a shortcut over a sandbank is thrown away, not costed) and waves that are too big.

Why this matters for the pitch: there is only one fuel calculation in the whole system. Route planning and throttle advice agree with each other to six decimal places, and we have an automated test that fails if they ever stop agreeing. A judge cannot find two numbers in our system that contradict each other, because there is only one place they come from.

Problem 2 — Breakdowns get discovered at sea instead of being planned at the dock Stage 1 working

What we do: learn what "normal" looks like for that particular engine across seven sensors, then flag anything that drifts away from it. Importantly, it also catches problems where every single sensor still looks fine on its own, but the combination is one a healthy engine never produces.

We are honest about what it can't do yet, and the code enforces it. In its first ~24 months the system can say which sensor is drifting — "coolant temperature is rising" — but it physically cannot name a broken part or predict a repair date. If any part of the program tries to output that, the data rules reject it and the request fails. Naming a part needs a history of real breakdowns to learn from, which a newly installed unit simply doesn't have yet.

Problem 3 — Without emissions proof, operators are locked out of green financing Working

What we do: produce a monthly report of carbon dioxide avoided, downloadable as a spreadsheet file — evidence an operator can email to the local government, to MARINA, or to a lender. It needs no extra sensors at all beyond what Problems 1 and 2 already use.

The report refuses to flatter us. CO₂ isn't predicted, it's basic chemistry: 2.68 kg of CO₂ per litre of diesel burned. The honest part is the comparison — we measure each trip against that same boat's own past trips on that same route, not against some fleet average. A trip with no past record still counts its fuel and its CO₂ in the totals, but contributes nothing to the "avoided" figure, and the report says how many trips that was. A month that burned more than before is reported as a negative number, not rounded up to zero.

Running through all of it — the line the AI is not allowed to cross Working

Emergency alarms — engine too hot, oil pressure too low, battery failing — are simple fixed rules, completely separate from any AI. That part of the program loads no AI model at all, and we have a test that fails if anyone ever adds one. Give it the same sensor reading and it gives the same answer, every time, forever.

This is three things at once: an ethical safeguard, a legal requirement under maritime liability law, and simply the right engineering choice when a wrong answer could hurt someone.

4.4 — The five emergency alarms

Fixed limits anyone can check against an engine manual. Three of them only apply once the engine is actually running (above 400 RPM) — otherwise every healthy boat would set off two alarms while still tied up at the wharf, and crews would quickly learn to ignore the alarm.

AlarmTriggers atNote
Engine overheating105 °C or aboveAlways checked
Oil pressure too low100 kPa or belowOur original plan said "too high" — that isn't how a diesel's oil system fails. We changed it, wrote down why, and added a test so it can't drift back
Battery too low12.0 V or belowThe alternator isn't charging; navigation and radio are running on the battery alone
Battery too high15.8 V or aboveA failed regulator, which overcharges the battery and can damage instruments
Exhaust too hot580 °C or aboveOnly while running

4.5 — Quality habits worth mentioning if asked

  • One definition of every piece of data. The shapes of all our data are written once, and the screen's version is generated automatically from it — so the two halves can never drift apart.
  • Every sensor reading is checked before use — is it physically possible, is the timestamp sane, and has the sensor stopped moving? A sensor stuck at one value is treated as just as suspicious as one reading nonsense. Out-of-order and duplicate readings are rejected, not quietly reordered.
  • Every reading is stamped "simulator" in the data itself, and there is no way for it to accidentally claim otherwise.
  • The published program is deliberately tiny — 85 MB instead of 358 MB — which is what lets it run cheaply in the cloud. Every part respects that limit; the health checker was hand-written for exactly this reason.
  • 307 automated tests, all passing. They protect the promises, not just the code — for example, one test fails if weather ever stops slowing the boat down.

05The AI, explained

Four things we trained ourselves, plus Claude for writing sentences — and five important parts we deliberately did not use AI for. That split is our strongest argument: use AI where there is real data to learn from, physics where there is proven theory, and fixed rules where a wrong answer is dangerous.

Five words you'll hear, in plain English

XGBoost — a well-known method that learns by building thousands of small yes/no decision trees and combining them. Good at spotting patterns in tables of numbers.

ONNX — a small, portable file format for a finished trained model. It lets us run the model without installing the big, heavy training software.

PCA — a method that learns the normal relationships between several sensors at once, then measures how far a new reading sits from that normal pattern.

Held-out data — examples deliberately hidden from the model during training, kept back to test it fairly afterwards. Testing on data it already saw would flatter it.

Average error — how far off the model is, typically. Smaller is better.

The checker is the part worth bragging about the answer to "what if your AI is wrong?"

Every team at this event will have an AI writing text. The question a technical judge will ask is what stops it saying something wrong in public — and "we wrote a careful prompt" is not an answer.

Ours: the AI is handed a finished sentence and asked only to say it more naturally. What comes back is compared against the original before anyone sees it. Every number must match exactly — no rounding, no dropping the peso figure, no inventing a saving. It is not allowed to give an order ("reduce your speed" is rejected; "1650 RPM saves 2.1 L/h" is fine). It must fit on the screen. Fail any of those and the whole thing is thrown away — both languages together, because an English sentence sitting next to a Filipino one that says something different is worse than using our plain backup text for both.

And the AI is fenced in from the other direction too. A test scans the throttle calculator, the route planner, the health checker and the safety alarms, and fails if any of them ever starts using the AI. It sits at the very end of the chain, after every decision has already been made.

5.2 — What we deliberately did not use AI for

This half of the table is the stronger half in a technical conversation. Each row is somewhere using AI would have sounded more impressive and been worse.

The partWhat we used insteadWhy not AI
Working out how much engine power the conditions demand Standard boat-design physics: established formulas for hull drag, plus the extra effort caused by wind, waves, current, and passenger and cargo weight No public dataset exists where wind, current, waves or load actually change — in every dataset we could find, they are fixed. A model "trained" on that would completely ignore the very things our product reacts to, while confidently claiming to account for them. Every number in our formulas is named, sourced, and can be tuned per boat from that boat's own fuel meter.
Turning engine power into litres of fuel A published fuel curve for marine diesel engines Our training dataset is a gas turbine, not a diesel. At low power a gas turbine wastes about 7× its best fuel rate; a diesel only about 1.5×. Copying the turbine's curve would have overstated our fuel savings by roughly five times, in our own favour — so we don't. A test keeps our diesel curve inside published limits so it can never slide back.
Emergency alarms Five fixed limits Behaviour in an emergency has to be identical every single time and checkable by a human. It loads no AI, remembers nothing between checks, and a test makes sure it never will.
Calculating CO₂ Chemistry: 2.68 kg of CO₂ per litre burned An "AI emissions model" that just multiplied litres by a fixed number and called it a prediction would be the least defensible thing we could build. The accuracy of the report comes openly from the accuracy of the fuel calculation.
Distances and bearings on the map Standard navigation maths for distance over a curved Earth These have exact answers. There is nothing to learn.

5.3 — Decisions we should be ready to defend

  • We tested the fuel model on whole engine conditions it had never seen, not on random rows. The dataset covers every combination systematically, so picking random rows to test on would leave almost-identical examples on both sides — any model would score near-perfectly without having learned anything. We held back 332 of 1,326 wear states entirely.
  • We tested the weather model on later dates, never on random hours. Weather an hour apart is nearly identical, so random testing would effectively let the model peek at the answer.
  • RPM is not used to predict fuel. In the dataset it barely changes within a power setting — it's just another way of writing down engine load. Including it would add something that looks useful and contributes nothing. We still show RPM and still give advice in RPM, because that's the lever the captain's hand is on.
  • The saved model is checked against the trained one. Our training program refuses to save the small portable file if its answers differ from the original by more than a hair. The measured difference is about a millionth — ordinary rounding. So the model we ship is provably the one our tests approved.
  • How hard the engine is worked never affects the health score. A test enforces this. Working an engine hard is normal use, not a fault — if it raised the score, every busy crossing would light up the health warning and the crew would learn to ignore it.
  • Everything degrades instead of breaking. Missing fuel model → assumes a healthy engine, lowers its confidence, and says so on every answer. Missing weather model → falls back to a simple weather pattern, and says so. Missing AI key → uses our plain backup sentence, and says so. Can't save data → keeps it in memory, and the report says so.

09Where our documents are wrong

Fourteen places our writing and our code disagree, worst first. The top four damage the honesty we've built the whole pitch on.

1. Our "no hardware" statement points at an empty folder Critical

The README says every sensor reading comes from a simulator in a particular folder. That folder is empty. Eleven lines further down, the same README lists the folder as "not built yet" — so the file contradicts itself on one page. DATA.md repeats the same claim.

This is the hardware declaration the competition requires. It is the first thing a doubting judge checks, and the one paragraph where being wrong looks like hiding something rather than being out of date.

A second mistake rides along: DATA.md says the simulator works by replaying the navy dataset. It doesn't — it generates readings from a statistical model that deliberately matches the health checker. That's a better story than replaying data, and it's why opening the throttle raises two sensors together without setting off an alarm. It just isn't the story the document tells.

Fix — 10 minutes, no coding Point both documents at the real files. Ready-made replacement wording is in section 10. Keep the claim about live weather — that part is completely true.

2. Three documents disagree about a dataset we cite Critical

DATA.md says a NASA dataset was used to pre-train our health checker. DEVIATIONS.md says the exact opposite in plain words — neither dataset trains it — and warns that the citation must not suggest otherwise. The deck agrees with DEVIATIONS; PROJECT_OVERVIEW agrees with DATA.md.

Claiming to have used a dataset we didn't is exactly the kind of thing our honesty list exists to prevent, and DEVIATIONS already flags it against itself. Two of our four documents just never got the correction.

Fix Change the DATA.md entry to "considered for training, not used — the checker learns each boat's own normal instead", and make PROJECT_OVERVIEW say the same. Use identical wording in all four files.

12The hard questions, and our answers

Seven questions a technical judge is likely to ask. The answers are already true — this is just where to find them.

"Did you build the advanced forecasting model you promised?"

No, and the reason is structural. That kind of model predicts the future by looking at what just happened. Our route planner has nothing to look at — it's asked for a path before the boat leaves the wharf, knowing only where you're going and when you're leaving. Without recent readings to work from, that model collapses into "predict the weather from a place and a time", which is exactly what we built instead. Then we raced nine predictions against a simple historical-average table on six months of real weather we'd held back, and the simple table won five of the nine. We shipped the losing scoreboard rather than quietly tuning it away.

"What stops your AI from saying something wrong in public?"

Claude is handed a finished sentence and asked only to say it better. What comes back is compared against the original: every number must match exactly, and it isn't allowed to give an order. Fail either check and it's thrown away, our plain sentence is used instead, and the screen records which one the captain is reading. That's the difference between having an AI in the product and having an AI in the decision — we only have the first.

"You trained a boat fuel model on a gas turbine."

Yes, and we only take one thing from it: how much extra fuel a worn engine burns compared with a healthy one at the same load. Not the fuel amounts, and — importantly — not the shape of its low-power curve. A gas turbine wastes about 7× its best fuel rate at low power; a diesel about 1.5×. Copying that curve would have overstated our savings by about five times, in our own favour. There's a test keeping our diesel curve inside published limits so it can never drift back.

"How do I know your route savings and throttle savings agree?"

Because there's only one fuel calculation, and a test enforces it. The route planner costs every segment using the same calculation the throttle advice uses; a single-segment route agrees with the throttle advice to six decimal places. Emissions use the same fuel figure again. You cannot find two numbers in this system that disagree, because there's only one place they come from.

"What happens when your model is wrong?"

Safety doesn't ask it. The emergency alarms load no model, remember nothing between checks, and a test makes sure they never will. Engine over 105 °C, oil pressure under 100 kPa, battery outside 12.0–15.8 V, exhaust over 580 °C — all readable off the page and checkable against an engine manual. A sensor that isn't reporting is marked "not checked", never assumed safe. Same reading, same answer, forever.

"Your predictive maintenance can't actually predict anything yet."

Correct — and our code enforces that. In stage one it can say which sensor is drifting and physically cannot name a part or a date; the data rules reject any attempt. Stage two needs a history of real breakdowns, which a newly installed unit doesn't have. Say this before they do.

"Show me the hardware."

There isn't any, and we declare it on slide 4. The kit is fully specified — rev counter, exhaust probe, motion sensor, GPS, fuel meter, weather feed — and what our models read is exactly what that kit would produce. This is a working system waiting for sensors, not a mock-up. Fix item 1 in section 09 first, or this answer points at an empty folder.
